Why Did I Decide On Pink For My Substack?

Ok, storytime. Before AI Meets Girlboss was pink, it almost wasn’t. I sat with hot pink for two weeks before I let myself commit to it. Not because I didn’t like it. Because I was scared of what it said about me.

Pink felt soft. Decorative. Like the color version of not being taken seriously in a room full of people who default to navy and charcoal when they want to signal “serious brand about AI.”

I kept asking myself: can a brand about systems, strategy, and AI actually wear hot pink and still be credible?

Here’s what changed my mind. I stopped asking “do I like this color” and started asking “what does this color need to say about me.”

Hot pink doesn’t say soft. In the right system, it says confident, daring, impossible to ignore in a feed full of beige. That’s a documented psychological response — people physically respond differently to high-energy colors like hot pink and red than to muted or cool tones. Once I understood the research behind it, picking pink stopped being a risk and started being a decision.

Color Is Not Decoration. It’s a Communication Decision.

Most Substack creators treat brand colors like a decoration choice. Pick something you like, make sure it’s not ugly, move on.

The problem with that is your readers aren’t neutral. They’re processing your visual brand before they’ve read a word, and color is the first signal they receive. Research in color-brand psychology consistently shows that when a brand’s visual identity matches the personality it’s trying to communicate, trust and recognition go up. When they don’t match, something feels off — even if the reader can’t name why.

There’s an academic framework that maps this directly. Marketing researcher Jennifer Aaker, now a professor at Stanford’s Graduate School of Business, developed a model identifying five measurable dimensions of brand personality — Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, and Ruggedness.

Later research by Labrecque and Milne connected those five dimensions to specific color profiles, establishing that color isn’t just aesthetic preference. It’s a personality signal your brand is broadcasting whether you’ve thought about it or not.

The practical takeaway: your brand color should match your brand personality, not your personal taste.

What Each Color Actually Says.

Here’s the map. Every color listed below has documented psychological associations backed by both consumer behavior research and the Aaker dimension framework. This is not “yellow is cheerful because it feels sunny.” These are patterns verified across studies.

Hot Pink | Vibrant Red | Bright Orange

Signal: Excitement. Daring, bold, spirited, energetic.

These are high-wavelength colors that physically increase alertness and urgency. Brands using these are saying: we are impossible to ignore and we know it.

Examples: Coca-Cola, Red Bull, Nickelodeon, Barbie.

Soft Blue | Sage Green | Warm Beige | Earth Tones

Signal: Sincerity. Down-to-earth, honest, warm, dependable.

These colors borrow their credibility from nature — they read as real rather than performative.

Examples: Patagonia, Whole Foods, most wellness brands.

Navy | Deep Corporate Blue | White

Signal: Competence. Reliable, intelligent, in control.

Blue is universally linked to institutional trust. If your brand needs to communicate authority before anything else.

Examples: IBM, Chase Bank, LinkedIn.

Deep Purple | Metallic Gold or Silver | Black

Signal: Sophistication. Glamorous, exclusive, expensive-feeling.

These colors have the longest cultural association with status and luxury.

Examples: Chanel, Rolex, Tiffany & Co.

Dark Brown | Forest Green | Charcoal

Signal: Ruggedness. Tough, outdoorsy, built to last.

These colors borrow credibility from stone, wood, and earth. Durability before style.

Examples: Jeep, Timberland, Caterpillar.

How Many Colors Do You Actually Need?

You’ll need three to five colors. One is not enough and eight is just too much.

Here’s the working rule I’d give for a Substack brand palette, using the 60-30-10 framework designers actually use:

Primary color (60%): This is the dominant color — your backgrounds, your brand header, the color that shows up most. It sets the overall mood. This should map directly to your brand personality dimension.

Secondary color (30%): Your supporting color. It complements your primary and appears in accents, section headers, and design elements. It can reinforce the same dimension or introduce a secondary personality signal.

Accent color (10%): The pop. Used strictly for CTAs, links, highlights, anything you need someone to click or notice. This should contrast clearly with your primary.

Neutrals (two — a light and a dark): Not technically a “brand color” but essential. One light neutral (soft white, cream, warm ivory) for backgrounds and breathing room. One dark neutral (charcoal, near-black) for body text. Without these, your palette has nowhere to rest.

Think of Color as a Wheel, Not a List.

An easy way to work with color is to stop thinking of it as a list of options and start thinking of it as a wheel.

Every hue sits on that circle in the order they fall in the visible spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, back to red. The reason this matters for branding is that where a color sits relative to another color on that circle predicts how the two will feel together, before you ever put them side by side.

Three relationships do most of the work:

Adjacent. Colors next to each other on the wheel — red and orange, blue and green. They share an undertone, which is why they feel calm together rather than competitive.

Opposite. Colors directly across the wheel from each other — red and green, blue and orange, purple and yellow. These are “complementary,” and the term is literal: each one makes the other look more saturated and alive by contrast.

Equally spaced. Three points spread evenly around the wheel, with no two close together and no two direct opposites. More visual range, more to manage.

That’s the whole vocabulary. The four schemes below are just four different ways of pointing at the same wheel.

The Four Color Schemes That Work for Substack Brands.

Once you know your primary color, you need to know how to build around it. This is where most people either get it right or blow it. The four standard color schemes — the ones designers actually use — each create a different visual effect and suit a different brand energy.

1. Monochromatic.

One color, multiple shades and tints of the same hue, with neutrals layered in to anchor it. AI Meets Girlboss’ core hue is monochromatic in spirit — hot pink as the primary, blush as the tint, dark pink as the shade — but a strict monochromatic palette stays inside that one hue family, and neutrals like cream and charcoal sit outside it even when they’re doing supporting work.

Monochromatic palettes are the most cohesive and the easiest to keep consistent. The risk is feeling flat if you don’t vary the saturation enough.

Good for: brands with one very strong personality dimension who want total visual unity.

Monochromatic palette. Image generated in ChatGPT Images 2.0 by AI Meets Girlboss.
2. Analogous.

Two or three colors sitting next to each other on the color wheel — think pink + orange + red, or blue + teal + green. These schemes feel harmonious and natural because the colors share undertones. They’re cooler or warmer depending on which side of the wheel you pull from, but always less punchy than a complementary pairing.

Good for: Sincerity and Ruggedness brands, wellness and coaching, anything that needs to feel organic rather than designed.

Analogous palette. Image generated in ChatGPT Images 2.0 by AI Meets Girlboss.

lue, blue-green, and green sit side by side on the wheel. The trio reads as natural and tranquil, the kind of palette that evokes water and forest rather than a designed brand. This is the analogous instinct working correctly: nothing here competes for attention.

3. Complementary.

Two colors directly opposite each other on the color wheel — pink and green, blue and orange, purple and yellow. High contrast, high energy, immediately attention-grabbing. This is the scheme behind most Excitement brands. The risk: if both colors are saturated, it’s a lot. One should dominate (60%), the other should accent (10%).

Good for: brands that need to stand out in a crowded feed and aren’t afraid of contrast.

Complementary palette. Image generated in ChatGPT Images 2.0 by AI Meets Girlboss.

In this example, purple sits near-opposite lime-green on the wheel, which is exactly why the pairing has this much tension. Purple carries the weight as the dominant 60%, lime is the jolt that makes it impossible to scroll past. If you try this combination, lime cannot be your primary. It only works as the smallest, sharpest accent — the second you let it lead, the palette stops reading as confident and starts reading as a highlighter.

4. Triadic.

Three colors are equally spaced around the color wheel — a more complex, vibrant scheme. This one requires restraint: it works when one color dominates and the other two support. Without hierarchy, a triadic palette reads as chaotic.

Good for: Competence brands that want more visual range, or multi-product brands with distinct content categories to color-code.

Triadic palette. Image generated in ChatGPT Images 2.0 by AI Meets Girlboss.

In this example yellow, mauve, and blue-green sit at roughly equal distances around the wheel — none of them are adjacent, none of them are direct opposites. That spacing is what gives this palette more range than a two-color scheme without it collapsing into chaos, as long as one of the three is doing most of the work and the other two are supporting it, not competing with it.

The most common mistake on Substack: picking a complementary or triadic scheme for the visual excitement, then applying every color at equal weight. Your accent should be exactly that — an accent. The drama is in the ratio, not just the colors.
