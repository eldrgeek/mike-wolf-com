I swear I had my brand rules written down.

I swear I had my brand rules written down… on sticky notes, PDFs, a Google Sheet I’d half-abandoned, an image folder on my desktop, and two different Notion pages. I knew what my brand looked like. Getting AI to know what my brand looked like meant 20 minutes of hunting before I could prompt anything.

I’m going to give you permission to stop beating yourself up about your AI image results. The problem usually isn’t that you haven’t thought about your visual consistency. It’s that what you’ve figured out is scattered across six different places and you have to reassemble it every single time you open a new generation session.

For me the shift came in stages. I built a character library first, a folder of consistent my character’s - Pinkie’s - reference images I could upload at the start of a session. That helped. Then I did a collab with Kristina Bogović and Calder Quinn from AI But Make It Intimate on character sheets, one image that locked the character, instead of a messy folder.

And I realised: I was only ever uploading one or two images anyway. So why not make one image that has everything in it for my brand?

That’s a brand reference sheet. And building it was the moment my AI image generation sessions stopped feeling like prep work.

Every Big Brand Has a Rulebook. You Do Too, Now.

You’ve seen brand guides without knowing that’s what they were.

The PDF that tells every designer, agency, and intern which shade of red Coca-Cola uses. The document that specifies exactly how far the Nike swoosh has to sit from the edge of a layout. The file that every person touching the brand gets before they touch it.

These documents exist because consistency at scale requires shared reference points. Without one, the brand fragments. Different blues. Logo stretched on a slide. Wrong font. You’ve seen what that looks like — even on big brands who slip.

Here’s what changed for you: you’re not the only one creating content for your brand anymore. AI is.

And AI has exactly the same problem as a new junior designer who joined the team without a briefing. It will make something. It might even make something competent. But it won’t make something yours, because it doesn’t know what yours looks like.

What Goes Into A Brand Reference Sheet.

There’s no single right format, but there is a minimum that works.

At the core:

your color palette with hex codes,

your typography,

your character or mascot if you have one,

your logo,

3-4 visual style references that capture texture, mood, and proportion.

Beyond that:

an icon library,

character poses,

world-building props,

brand easter eggs, and anything that makes your work unmistakably yours at a glance.

How much do you actually know about your brand?

Before you generate anything, answer one question: how much do you actually know about your brand? Not what you aspire to. What you have right now, specifically, that you could hand to someone and they’d know what to do with it.

Your answer determines which of the three brand reference sheet build paths is yours, and which prompt you’ll use from this post. I built them to match you exactly where you are.

Path 1: Baby Flamingo: You have taste but no decisions yet. Colors, fonts, visual direction: nothing is locked.

Path 2: Junior Flamingo: You have a vibe but no system. There’s a look to your content, but you couldn’t brief it to someone else.

Path 3: Expert Flamingo: Your brand is developed. You have hex codes, fonts, a character or mascot. You’re documenting, not discovering.

One More Thing Before You Pick Your Tool.

I tested the same Path 3 prompt in both ChatGPT Images 2.0 and Gemini. Worth telling you what happened.

ChatGPT produced a result I was happy with on the first run. Uploaded my style references, gave it a clear brief, got Pinkie, the flamingo, the palette, the icons — all in one 16:9 sheet. Around 25 minutes total.

ChatGPT Images 2.0 version #1 with the simple Path 3 prompt.

Gemini did not work the same way. Version 1 looked like something produced with zero brand training and no visual instincts. Version 2 — which I only got after feeding it my full written brand guide — was better. Still not at the level of the ChatGPT output. Still required significantly more prompting to get there.

Generated in Gemini, version #2 after adding a 600-word prompt to the original prompt.

Here’s what I figured out: ChatGPT Images 2.0 reads visual references. Gemini reads written rules.

Upload images to ChatGPT and it interprets texture, style, proportion, and atmosphere from what it sees. Gemini needs the rules described in language — every color with its hex code, every stylistic rule articulated. The prompt I ended up using for Gemini’s Version 2 was almost 600 words long.

Neither approach is wrong. But they are different tools that speak different languages. Knowing that before you start will save you a lot of frustrated regenerating.

The Three Brand Reference Sheet Prompts.

One rule before you copy any of these: your prompt is only as good as what you upload with it — and which tool you’re using.

For Path 1, you’ll upload nothing. The prompt does the asking.

For Path 2, collect 5–8 existing visuals that feel like you — screenshots, mood boards, references you admire, even if they’re not yours.

For Path 3 on ChatGPT: gather your logo, brand assets, and existing style references.

On Gemini: you’ll also need your brand rules written out in words — colors with hex codes, style descriptions, font names, all of it articulated. The Gemini prompt is longer by design.

Path 1: Baby Flamingo — Starting From Scratch.

Use this if you have ideas for your brand but haven't made decisions yet. It will interview you first and then suggest a brand direction.

Note: this prompt will not give you a finished brand — it locks in the direction that's been vaguely forming in your head.

I want to create a consistent visual reference image for my brand that I can use to generate on-brand assets — covers, infographics, charts, and video thumbnails — in future conversations. Help me build it.

First, ask me one question at a time to understand what I'm working with:

— Do I already have a visual brand (logo, colors, fonts, existing assets), or am I building from scratch?

— If I have assets: ask me to share my website URL, any existing visuals, and any brand colors or fonts I already know.

— If I'm starting from scratch: help me discover my visual direction. Ask about my audience, what I want my brand to communicate, and what I want people to feel when they see it. Then suggest 2–3 visual directions with example color palettes and ask me to pick.

— In either case: ask me for 2–3 examples of brands or publications I admire visually.

Once you understand my brand, generate one reference image that locks my visual style: color palette, typography logic, texture, layout feel, and overall aesthetic. This image will be my style anchor for all future generations.

Generate 3 variations. Do not change the style between them — only vary the composition slightly. I will choose one as my permanent reference.

Path 2: Junior Flamingo — Vibes First.

Use this if you have visual inspiration — a Pinterest board, brands you admire, styles you're drawn to — but no rules or decisions yet. Upload 5–8 reference images before sending this prompt.

AI will interpret what it sees and suggest a visual direction. Note: this prompt won't produce a finished brand reference sheet — it gives you a starting point to react to and refine.

I'm building a visual brand reference sheet for my brand: [Brand Name].

I don't have a locked visual system yet, but I have a strong sense of what my brand feels like. I'm going to share some reference images with you.

Based on those references:

— Interpret the visual direction you see emerging. What's the aesthetic? What's the mood? What's the underlying visual logic?

— Then build me a brand reference sheet that captures that direction. Make creative decisions where I haven't made them yet. I want to see what you think my brand could look like at its most fully realized.

The reference sheet should include: logo suite or wordmark, character world (create characters that fit the brand's feel), core color palette, icon library (6–8 icons), typography, and brand settings or textures.

Make the aspect ratio 16:9. One cohesive landscape board.

I'll tell you what to keep, change, or refine after I see it.
Path 3: Expert Flamingo — Developed Brand (ChatGPT).

Use this if your brand rules and assets already exist but live in six different places — a Google Sheet, a Notion page, a desktop folder, your head.

This prompt consolidates everything into one reference sheet you can upload every session. Of the three paths, this one gets you closest to a finished, ready-to-use output.

Generate a visual brand reference sheet for my Brand: [Brand Name].

The brand reference sheet should have:

— My character and brand mascot: [Character name + mascot name]
— My character in 4 poses ([character name] only)
— My brand mascot in 4 poses ([mascot name] only)
— My brand colors
— My logo
— Color palette with hex codes
— Icon library (6 icons): [list your icons or describe the style]
— Font: [Font name] — it should read: [Your brand name]

I am attaching visual style references and brand rules for you to follow and incorporate.

Make the aspect ratio 16:9. One cohesive landscape board. Do not add anything that isn't in this brief.

One thing to expect on Path 3 in ChatGPT: it can handle up to 8 reference images before it gets overloaded. If your brand is visually complex and you have more than 8 references, batch them — run the prompt twice with different reference sets and combine what you get.

Path 3: Expert Flamingo — Developed Brand (Gemini Adaptation).

Use this if you're on Path 3 but working in Gemini. The output is the same — a consolidated reference sheet from your existing assets — but Gemini needs your brand rules written in language rather than uploaded as images.

The prompt is longer by design. The more specific your descriptions, the closer you get to a finished result on the first run.

A professional, ultra-clean 16:9 brand reference sheet and visual style guide layout for the brand "[Brand Name]".

The entire layout is presented on a single cohesive canvas with a [describe your background — e.g. warm off-white, ivory-toned paper] background showing [describe texture if relevant]. The canvas features [describe any watercolor or texture elements with hex codes].

The overall artistic style is [describe your illustration style — e.g. high-end mixed-media fashion illustration combining sharp, clean pencil linework with fluid watercolor washes].

The sheet is neatly divided into organized, clean grid modules containing the following brand assets:

1. BRAND LOGO & BRAND STATEMENT: [Describe your logo format and brand statement text exactly as it should appear.]

2. CHARACTER POSES ("[Character Name]"): A horizontal lineup of 4 full-body illustration poses (Front, Left profile, Back, Right profile). [Describe your character's appearance, hair, expression, and outfit in detail — proportions, colors, textures, specific garment details.]

3. MASCOT POSES ("[Mascot Name]"): A horizontal sequence of 4 distinct poses directly below the character lineup. [Describe each pose. Describe the illustration style — watercolor, pencil, etc.]

4. TYPOGRAPHY: A clean text module showing: [Primary font name] for headings, [Secondary font name] for body copy, [Accent font name] as handwritten accent — displaying the text "[Brand Name]" with a full Aa Bb Cc character map.

5. COLOR PALETTE: [Number] horizontal rectangular swatches with hex codes labeled clearly: "[Color name] [hex code]" for each, showing tint steps.

6. ICON SET: A neat 2x3 grid of 6 minimalist icons: [describe each icon individually]. All icons in [describe your color/style].

Crisp layout, editorial graphic design style, symmetrical grid, sharp details, 16:9 aspect ratio.

The more specific your descriptions, the better your output. Treat it like briefing a designer who has never seen your brand before. Because in Gemini’s case, that’s exactly what’s happening.
