Designing a Logo and Discovering One Are Two Different Things.

Most creators open Canva, type in their brand name, pick a style they saw someone else use, and call it done. The result may look professional at first glance, but it’s completely forgettable the moment you close the tab.

Here’s the difference.

Designing asks: What should represent me? You’re building something from nothing.

Discovering asks: What already does? The answer is usually already there, in the image you keep reaching for, the metaphor you keep using, the tension that defines why your brand exists at all.

The best logos that live in people’s heads are almost never designed. They’re exaggerated versions of something that was already there.

If you’re unsure what represents you, start with building your brand voice before a visual identity. The full framework, is in Turn Your Personality Into Your Brand Voice. It’s also a good first step before today’s logo workflow, or a useful next step if you want more depth after you’ve tried the prompts here.

Five Real Substack Logos. Here’s What They Actually Do.

Before you generate anything, I want to show you what a working logo looks like in practice. I studied five real Substack publication logos asking one question about each: is this doing brand work or decorative work?

Brand work: remove the name. Would the right person still recognise what this stands for?

Decorative work: the logo is there, but it could belong to any one of a thousand newsletters.

You don’t need to have it figured out before you start generating. In fact, reacting to options is one of the fastest ways to discover what you actually want. What repels you is often more useful than what attracts you.

Prompt 1 — The Quick Start.

What this does: Give ChatGPT your Substack URL and a few basic inputs. It reads your profile and generates five concepts from what it finds. This is the fastest way to get something on screen — which is the point.

What to expect: Decent but probably not distinctive. This is a first date, not a marriage. You’re not looking for “the one” — you’re looking for reactions. What direction feels closer? What colors feel right? What shapes make you wince? Both responses are useful. And remember: whatever you land on will live on your Substack, Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook profile. It follows you everywhere.

How to use it: Run it once. Note what you feel. Use those reactions to build round two.

Research my Substack page called [YOUR PUBLICATION NAME]. 
It's a page about [YOUR TOPIC]. [YOUR SUBSTACK URL]

Design 5 logo variations for my page that reflects my brand 
and will make me recognizable.

Must have elements:
- Color: [YOUR HEX CODES]
- Mascot / symbol: [YOUR BRAND ELEMENT]
- Style: [YOUR AESTHETIC — e.g. fashion, editorial, minimal]

Prompt 2 — Round Two With Constraints.

What this does: You’ve seen round one and you have reactions. Now you add more specific direction and run again. This is where the output lifts. The more constraints you give — a font direction, a layout rule, what to avoid, which version was closest — the better the result.

What to expect: Noticeably sharper and more specific than round one. Still a starting point, but now you’re converging.

How to use it: Take your closest direction from round one and add constraints:

Take version [X] from round one as the closest direction.

Now refine and generate 5 new versions with these additional constraints:
- Typography: [e.g. clean geometric sans-serif / bold display / no handwriting]
- Layout: [e.g. icon above the name / name and icon side by side]
- What to avoid: [e.g. gradients, anything corporate, cartoon styles]
- The icon must work at 32x32px — simple enough to read as a profile photo

The Three Logo Discovery Approaches.

The best mark isn’t something you invent. It’s something you find. These aren’t sequential steps — they’re three different lenses. Most brands will connect strongly with one. Start there.

3 approached to build your brand logo. Image generated in ChatGPT by AI Meets Girlboss
Approach 1 — The Fact.

What it is: Something biographical, structural, or numerical that is specific to you and impossible to copy. Dates. Credentials. A founding year. A proprietary method. The logo encodes the proof — reading the mark is reading evidence of what you’ve built.

Who it’s for: Authority-first brands where credibility is the core product. If readers follow you because of what you’ve earned, built, or verified, this gives you something no competitor can replicate.

How to sit with it: List your five most specific facts that define your identity. Then ask: which of these are so distinctively mine that seeing them on a logo would make the right person nod?

Approach 2 — The Symbol.

What it is: Something you were already using before you had a brand. A metaphor, a creature, a word, an image. The logo was already there — doing brand work informally. This approach makes it official.

Who it’s for: Feeling-first, personality-driven brands. If readers follow you for how you think and who you are, the symbol approach gives you a mark that doesn’t represent you. It is you, just drawn.

How to sit with it: Go back to when you first started talking about your work. What image kept coming up? What did you call things before you had proper names for them? If you had to draw one image that felt like your brand without any text — what would it be? Give yourself time with this one. The answer isn’t always immediate.

Approach 3 — The Tension.

What it is: Your brand lives between two worlds that weren’t supposed to go together. The logo’s job is to hold that tension visible — to show that you’re not one thing or the other, but the productive space between them.

Who it’s for: Reframe brands. If the whole point of your publication is that two things that don’t usually go together actually belong together — and that combination is where the value lives — the tension approach gives you a mark that embodies your premise.

How to sit with it: Finish this sentence: “My brand is [X] meets [Y].” Then ask: where does that tension show up visually? In the color palette? In the typography? In the way shapes meet? The mark doesn’t have to explain the tension. It just has to carry it.

I Tested the Logo Discovery Skill on My Own Logo. Here’s What Happened.

I ran my own logo discovery prompt on myself — not a demo, an actual session. Starting point: a handwritten "AI" on a blush watercolor brush stroke with a pink flamingo, built in Photoshop six months earlier when I needed something fast. It had charm. It had no system. It didn't scale.

Original AI Meets Girlboss logo. Created in Photoshop.

Step 1 — Route discovery. I landed on The Tension immediately. AI Meets Girlboss is two worlds in the name. That framing changed what I was asking the mark to do — hold the friction visible, not explain it.

Step 2 — Reference images. I shared three logos I was drawn to. All three had the same thing: geometric simplicity with a structural twist. I hadn’t noticed the pattern until I put them side by side. The process surfaced what I was actually asking for.

Step 3 — First concepts. ChatGPT generated three directions. The geometric flamingo was the one — clean, scalable, and it worked at small size immediately.

1st version of AI Meets Girlboss logo set. Image generated in ChatGPT by AI Meets Girlboss

Step 4 — The surprise. The mark was structurally strong but wasn’t telling a story yet. When the prompt asked about symbolism, something clicked: a small grey baby flamingo nested beside the pink geometric mark. Grey to pink. The brand origin story encoded in one composed mark. I didn’t plan that. The process pulled it out.

2nd version of AI Meets Girlboss logo set. Image generated in ChatGPT by AI Meets Girlboss

Step 5 — The system question. I didn't replace the circular Pinkie mark I use for my publication's logo. I kept it as the Substack channel icon and used the geometric mark as the publication header. But I did update the circle — same font, lighter background, and the small geometric flamingo added as a connecting detail. Two marks, doing different jobs, now speaking the same language. One brand family.

When You Know You’ve Found Your Logo.

You’ll know when the reaction shifts from “that looks good” to “that’s it.”

That’s different from liking an option. It’s the feeling that the logo was already yours, and someone just drew it. As a solopreneur — where you are the brand — the mark has to feel like that. Not just professional. Yours.

If you don’t know what your logo is yet, that’s not a design problem. It’s a question you haven’t asked properly. The discovery takes as long as it takes. But when the right mark shows up, you won’t evaluate it. You’ll recognise it.
