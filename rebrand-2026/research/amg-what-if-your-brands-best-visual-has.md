There’s a visual default in AI content. You’ve seen it a hundred times. The glowing robot hand reaching toward a human. The neural network floating in deep space. The blue-tinted cityscape from a future that doesn’t exist yet.

These images all do the same thing: they illustrate the topic. AI = futuristic. So the visuals look futuristic. It’s literal. And it makes every AI newsletter look identical.

Wilbert Kramer looked at all of that and did the opposite.

He launched Decode: AI in late 2025, a newsletter for marketers and entrepreneurs who want to build durable AI skills without the noise. And then, just two months in, he rebranded. Not because the foundation was broken, but because the rest of the house needed to match it.

His visuals? Cute, expressive, 3D cartoon objects that smile at you. Not a robot in sight. Not a single glowing circuit.

Because Wilbert doesn’t illustrate his topic. He illustrates his tone. And that difference (literal versus metaphorical) is the single most underused move in Substack branding right now.

Your Visual Style Is a Stance, Not a Skin

I ran the Visual Distinctiveness Test on Wilbert’s brand before this interview. His strongest dimensions are Concrete (5/5), Simple (4/5), and Credible (4/5). That’s a rare combination in AI content, where most creators over-index on Unexpected or Emotional and forget to be clear.

Decode: AI Substack channel through Pinkie’s opinionated branding filter. (Generated with ChatGPT by AI Meets Girlboss based on an actual screenshot of Decode AI)

When I asked Wilbert why those dimensions show up so clearly, his answer revealed the entire philosophy behind the brand.

He’d spent a year in the loudest possible corner of AI. Following every launch. Writing about every tool. The pace was relentless. And when he stepped back to build something of his own, he knew exactly what he didn’t want to replicate.

“I wanted to bring peace in the AI landscape,” Wilbert told me. Especially for marketers, whose job was already an ever-changing and challenging one before AI entered the picture. He wanted Decode: AI to feel like a deep breath in a room full of people shouting.

That intention drove every visual decision that followed. And here’s the key: it drove him away from his subject, not toward it.

Think about what Wilbert didn’t do. He didn’t google “AI visuals” and pick something from page one. He didn’t look at what other AI newsletters were using and pick a variation. He asked himself: what does my reader need to feel? And the answer — calm, clarity, approachability — had nothing to do with robots, circuits, or glowing interfaces.

So he drew a smiling cartoon instead.

AI Meets Girlboss branding note: This is the trap I see constantly. Creators take their subject too literally when they design their visuals. Finance newsletter? Stock chart on the cover. Productivity writer? Checklist icon. AI creator? Robot hand. It’s literal, it’s safe, and it makes you invisible. Wilbert’s move was to illustrate his relationship to AI (calm, friendly, warm) rather than AI itself (futuristic, complex, technical). That’s the difference between a brand that blends in and a brand that stops your thumb. Your visuals should answer: “how do I want my reader to feel?” not “what is my newsletter about?”

Two Months In, and Already Rebuilding

Decode: AI launched in late 2025. By January 2026 Wilbert was already rebranding. That’s fast. Most creators wait until something feels truly broken. Wilbert moved before it could.

The cartoons had been there from day one. They were already the strongest element of the brand. But the logo, wordmark, and surrounding design elements were still basic. They hadn’t caught up with the ambition of the illustrations.

Cuddleable AI: When Your Metaphor Becomes Your Brand

Here’s the line from Wilbert that stopped me: “A cartoon about a somewhat complex or serious subject speaks directly to the heart. Through this imagery I am looking to simplify AI, make it cuddleable.”

Make it cuddleable. That’s not a word you hear in AI branding. And that’s exactly why it works.

Wilbert’s visual brand cues are object-driven metaphors, simplified illustrations for complex AI concepts, and diagrammatic storytelling. Every image features a 3D object with an expression (smiling, frowning, curious) placed against a soft Sandy Beach or Decode Blue background with intentional lighting and mood.

Notice: objects. Not screens. Not interfaces. Not humans at computers. Objects with faces. That’s a metaphorical leap most creators don’t make. When you’re writing about AI burnout, the literal image is a person slumped over a laptop. Wilbert’s version is a cartoon object that looks tired. When you’re writing about AI complexity, the literal image is a tangled flowchart. Wilbert’s version is a confused little object staring at something simple.

The metaphor does what the literal image can’t: it makes you feel something before your brain has time to categorize the topic as “hard” or “scary.”

The style started as a supporting role. Fun add-ons alongside the writing. But over time Wilbert noticed the images were doing more than decorating. They were carrying the story. A recent co-written post about AI burnout showed him how much stronger the message landed when the visuals didn’t just sit next to the text but told the narrative independently.

Post cover for “Using AI without burning out” on Decode: AI Substack channel. Photo credit: Wilbert Kramer.

“I am starting to think about the visuals not only as a fun add-on, but as a necessity to carry the story and deliver it in a stronger way,” Wilbert said. That shift — from illustration as accessory to illustration as argument — is the mark of a brand growing up.

AI Meets Girlboss branding note: Here’s the creative pattern worth stealing: when your visual style makes a philosophical claim “AI is approachable, not scary” the images stop being packaging and become content. A smiling cartoon object communicates Wilbert’s entire editorial stance faster than any tagline could. And because it’s metaphorical rather than literal, it’s ownable. Nobody else in the AI space looks like this. That’s the reward for going abstract when everyone else goes literal.

The Prompting Framework Behind One-Shot Illustrations

Wilbert’s illustrations look consistent because they are. He built a prompting framework in Nano Banana Pro that delivers what he calls “one shots”, images that land on the first generation without endless retries.

Post cover for “When to Use AI. And When Not To” on Decode: AI Substack channel. Photo credit: Wilbert Kramer.

Here’s what his framework locks down:

Style blend. Each prompt carries a hint of photorealism combined with cartoon styling. The objects are always 3D, always have an expression, and Wilbert defines the exact expression in the prompt: smiling at the camera, frowning, looking curious.

Lighting and mood. Every prompt specifies soft lighting and the precise emotional tone of the image. This isn’t vague “make it look nice.” It’s “this image should feel calm and slightly playful.”

Color control. Two base background colors: Sandy Beach and Decode Blue. Since the rebrand, every image also gets a hint of Decode Orange somewhere, a small but deliberate move that ties each new illustration back to the logo. Before the rebrand, the images leaned more pastel. The orange interplay was a conscious decision to make the visuals and the logo work as a system.

Text integration. Wilbert prompts the text that appears next to the cartoon directly in the image generation prompt. The text isn’t added afterward in Canva. It’s part of the composition from the start.

Post-processing. After generation, every image runs through Canva’s AI Image Upscaler at 4x or 8x. This is a small step that makes a big difference in how the final image feels on Substack, especially on high-resolution screens.

AI Meets Girlboss branding note: Wilbert’s framework is a masterclass in what I call “locked versus fluid” elements. His locked elements (3D style, expressions, soft lighting, base colors) create instant recognition. His fluid elements (the specific object, the expression, the mood, the text) give each image its own story. That’s a visual brand system, not just “a style I like.”

The Decode: AI Portrait Prompt:
Create a 3D cartoon-style illustration based on the uploaded photo.
A cartoon-style [DESCRIBE YOUR SCENARIO AND OBJECT — e.g. "woman sitting on a giant plush unicorn holding an iPad" / "man standing next to an oversized compass" / "person leaning against a glowing lantern the size of a chair"] [MAIN OBJECT — e.g. "unicorn" / "compass" / "lantern"] has a smiling face and looks directly at the camera.

Black hand-drawn doodle lines overlay the scene — slightly uneven, expressive sketchbook strokes drawn on the objects. Handwritten doodle text near the machine reads: [YOUR SHORT PHRASE — e.g. "I like your style." / "Let's decode this." / "Hold my coffee."]

Style: hybrid mix of realistic 3D product photography with cartoon doodle overlay. Background: [PICK A COLOR HEX OR NAME — Wilbert uses #c9e4eb] clean and minimal. Accent color: [PICK YOUR ACCENT — Wilbert uses #fb6200]. Lighting: soft, even studio light with gentle shadows. Mood: playful and pondering.

Aspect ratio: 1:1.
Tips for best results:

The object is where your personality lives. This is the metaphor part. Don’t pick something literal to your niche, pick something that captures the energy of your brand. Wilbert picked a unicorn for me, not a flamingo. Think metaphor, not icon.

The doodle text is your brand’s one-liner. What would your Substack say at a party? Keep it under 6 words.

The smiling face on the object is key. That’s Wilbert’s signature move, it’s what makes his illustrations feel warm rather than corporate. Don’t skip it.

Swap the background and accent colors to match your own brand palette. The structure stays the same, the colors make it yours.

Stop Illustrating Your Topic. Start Illustrating Your Tone.

I asked Wilbert the question I ask every Real Rebrand guest: what’s the smallest step a creator could take this week to make their visuals clearer and more intentional?

His answer was disarmingly simple. Start with your why. Not your color palette. Not your font pairing. Not your image generator. Your why.

What is your key differentiator? Often it comes from your own vision, your mission, or a lived experience. Take that differentiator and let it be the main inspiration for your imagery.

This is the practical version of the literal-versus-metaphor distinction. When you start with your topic, you end up with a stock photo of your category. When you start with your why, you end up with something only you could have made.

Wilbert Kramer’s why was exhaustion with AI noise and a desire to bring calm, that turned into cuddleable 3D cartoons. Elena | AI Product Leader’s why was the messiness of building products, that turned into a protagonist in motion. My why was reclaiming femininity in a masculine tech space, that turned into fashion illustrations.

None of us illustrate our topics literally. And that’s why each of these brands is immediately recognizable.

Your Turn! Find the Metaphor Your Brand Has Been Missing

Here’s the test for whether you’ve been illustrating your topic or your tone.

Wilbert turned complex AI into friendly cartoon objects. Not a literal representation of his subject, but a metaphor for his relationship with it. His style is unmistakably his. But the principle is universal: the most memorable brands don’t visualize what they do. They visualize how they feel about what they do.

This prompt helps you discover the object-as-metaphor that captures the energy of your Substack. Not your topic. Not your niche icon. The feeling behind your writing, turned into a single image.

The results are visual, shareable, and surprisingly revealing. If the AI hands you back something generic (a pen for a writing newsletter, a chart for a finance newsletter), that’s a signal: your description is too literal. Try again with more feeling and less category.

The Brand Metaphor Prompt:

Paste this into ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, or a similar image model.

You are a brand strategist and visual thinker. Help me discover a visual metaphor for my Substack brand.

Here's what I know:

My brand is called: [YOUR SUBSTACK NAME]
It's about: [ONE SENTENCE DESCRIPTION]
The feeling I want readers to have when they open my newsletter: [DESCRIBE THE EMOTION — e.g. "a deep breath after a hectic day" / "the thrill of finally understanding something complex" / "coffee with a smart friend who doesn't judge"]
What I'm actively rejecting in my niche (the visual cliché I don't want): [DESCRIBE IT — e.g. "the sci-fi robot hands" / "the hustle culture stock photos" / "the minimalist grid aesthetic everyone uses"]

Now:

1. Ask me 2 sharp follow-up questions that help you understand my brand's emotional core better. Wait for my answers before proceeding.

2. Then propose 3 visual metaphors — objects, scenes, or visual worlds — that could represent my brand. For each one:
   - Name the metaphor (e.g. "a lighthouse in fog" / "an oversized key in a small lock" / "a campfire with one empty chair")
   - Explain in one line WHY this metaphor fits my brand's feeling
   - Describe what it would look like as an image (style, color mood, composition)

3. None of the 3 should illustrate my topic literally. All 3 should be unexpected but feel immediately right.

4. After I pick one (or combine elements), give me a ready-to-paste image generation prompt I can use in ChatGPT, Gemini, or another image tool.
How to use it:

Fill in your brand details, spend the most time on the feeling line. That’s what drives the metaphor

Generate the image

Look at what comes back. Does it feel like your newsletter? If the object feels too literal or too random, adjust your feeling description and try again
